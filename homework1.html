Mathematics / 03

Method and interpretation

The browser uses modular arithmetic on the finite field F₁₇. “Division” means multiplying by the modular inverse.

Distinct points P ≠ R
λ = (y₂ − y₁) × (x₂ − x₁)⁻¹ mod 17
Point doubling P = R
λ = (3x₁²) × (2y₁)⁻¹ mod 17
Result x-coordinate
x₃ = (λ² − x₁ − x₂) mod 17
Result y-coordinate
y₃ = (λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁) mod 17
Special cases: the identity point ∞ satisfies P + ∞ = P. If P and R are inverses, P + R = ∞. Doubling a point with y = 0 also yields ∞. Fast scalar multiplication uses binary double-and-add rather than k consecutive additions.
01 · FREQUENCY

Uniformity has a domain

The full toy curve has 17 finite points plus ∞. The default G=(1,5) generates only 9 group elements: eight finite points and ∞. Coordinate histograms need not be uniform across 0–16.

02 · STRUCTURE

ECDLP, not hashing

For k in a suitable range, Q=kG is deterministic and hard to invert on real cryptographic curves. Consecutive keys satisfy (k+1)G = kG + G: a known algebraic relationship.

03 · LIMITATION

Statistics ≠ proof of security

Balanced bits, weak Pearson correlations, or large Hamming distances do not prove one-wayness. Avalanche properties are normally studied for hash functions, not required of elliptic-curve multiplication.

Final Remarks / 04

Conclusion

Summary of the experiment and statistical findings.

In this assignment, I explored how elliptic-curve scalar multiplication can be used to generate a public key from a private key. Using the toy elliptic curve y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17), I implemented point addition and scalar multiplication in JavaScript. For the private key k = 6 and base point G = (1,5), the resulting public key was Q = (5,8).

Beyond implementing the algorithm, I examined the statistical properties of the generated points, including coordinate distributions, bit frequencies, correlation, and Hamming distances. The results showed that the distribution of public-key coordinates is influenced by the mathematical structure of the elliptic curve and the selected base point. In particular, the repeated patterns observed in the experiment are explained by the small order of the base point.

One important observation is that statistical properties alone cannot determine whether a cryptographic algorithm is secure. For example, a low correlation between private keys and public-key coordinates does not necessarily imply that the relationship is unpredictable. Similarly, avalanche-like behavior is not a security requirement for elliptic-curve scalar multiplication in the same way that it is commonly studied for cryptographic hash functions.

Overall, this experiment helped me understand the connection between elliptic-curve mathematics and statistical analysis. It also highlighted the importance of considering the mathematical structure of cryptographic systems when interpreting statistical results. Although the toy curve is not suitable for real-world security, it provides a useful foundation for understanding the principles behind elliptic-curve cryptography used in Ethereum.
